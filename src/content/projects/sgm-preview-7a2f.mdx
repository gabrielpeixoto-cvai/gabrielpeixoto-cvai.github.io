## Abstract

Generative motion policies, from diffusion planners to vision-language-action models, enable robots to perform complex manipulation tasks from demonstrations but lack safety guarantees in dynamic environments with novel or moving obstacles. We present a safety framework that augments frozen generative policies with whole-arm collision avoidance through a unified control barrier function formulation. The framework supports both guided sampling during generation and model predictive control filtering during execution. These capabilities avoid moving obstacles without modifying the underlying generative model or assuming a specific robot embodiment. Unlike existing methods that restrict safety to the robot tip or rely on reactive braking, our approach anticipates obstacle motion and reroutes the whole arm while preserving task progress wherever feasible. Experiments with diffusion policies and flow-matching policies across multiple robot platforms show improved safe task completion over representative baselines, with the model predictive control filter the strongest configuration at 63.8% safe task completion.

## Method

The safety layer wraps a **frozen** generative motion policy and enforces whole-arm collision avoidance at execution time, without retraining. It is **generator-agnostic**: one interface wraps a diffusion planner, a flow-matching model, a Diffusion Policy, and a pi0.5 vision-language-action policy. It is **robot-agnostic**: one parametric forward-kinematics and capsule model instantiates the Franka Panda, UR5e, KUKA IIWA, and Kinova Gen3 from a per-robot description.

The robot and the obstacles are over-approximated by **capsules** (a segment plus a radius). The signed clearance `d(q)` is the exact closest-point capsule-to-capsule distance over all robot-obstacle pairs, and the barrier is the margin-shifted clearance `h(q) = d(q) - r_safe`. A conservative capsule radius makes a positive capsule clearance certify a positive mesh clearance. Because the decision input is the joint velocity and `q_{k+1} = q_k + qdot * dt`, the barrier has relative degree one, so a **first-order discrete control barrier function** is well posed and sufficient.

One CBF specification is applied at three points along the generation-to-execution axis:

- **Guidance** at generation time, shaping the sample against the obstacle by the barrier gradient.
- **Filter** at execution time, a model-predictive controller that re-solves a hard, state-feedback problem against the live joint state and the predicted (constant-velocity) obstacle at every tick.
- **Hybrid**, guidance then filter.

Only the execution-time filter repairs the command against the live state and the predicted obstacle, so it is the placement that yields a hard guarantee independent of how the plan was produced.

### Results

We evaluate in Robosuite on the PickPlace-can task. The robot picks a can and places it in a bin while a separate dynamic obstacle, an articulated arm of ten capsule links, moves into the workspace. The obstacle is grasp-triggered and executes one of sixteen curated motions that challenge each link and joint of the arm in turn, so checking only the end effector is structurally insufficient. The headline generator is a Diffusion Policy in absolute joint position, the loop runs at 20 Hz, and the obstacle is crossed with ten seeds.

The filter delivers the guarantee the baselines do not, holding penetration at **zero** across every whole-arm configuration on the designed matrix, while every faithful baseline penetrates the obstacle on a third to three quarters of episodes. The best cell, whole-arm coverage with obstacle prediction on a Diffusion Policy, reaches **63.8% safe task completion at zero penetration**, beating the strongest baseline by roughly 31 points and widening the gap on the harder stress set. The guarantee extends to the carried can through the post-grasp object capsule.

### Ablation: where to enforce safety

The same CBF specification is placed at three points along the generation-to-execution axis: **Guidance** at generation time, the execution-time **Filter** (a model-predictive controller), and **Hybrid**, guidance followed by the filter. Guidance alone shapes the sample against the obstacle pose available at generation time, but by execution the obstacle has moved and the guided plan is stale, so it still penetrates on a third of episodes. The execution-time filter re-solves against the live joint state and the predicted obstacle at every control tick and drops penetration to zero. Hybrid does not improve on the filter alone. ### Perception robustness

The headline experiments hand the filter the true obstacle pose. To measure what the safety layer is worth without one, we replace that oracle with a simulated depth pipeline. Two virtual depth cameras cast rays into the scene, and the returned points are assigned to the limbs of a simulated 3D body tracker whose per-landmark noise is calibrated against published Azure-Kinect-versus-motion-capture accuracy. We simulate the output of a commodity body tracker rather than one we built, deriving noisy landmarks from the true joint positions, and we fit a capsule per limb from the points assigned to it. One thing is still free and we state it plainly. Ground-truth geometry ids decide which returned points belong to the human at all, which is the same free segmentation the depth-space literature this descends from also assumes, since that literature does not need to know what the obstacle is.

*Four honesty notes travel with every number in this section. The shipped configuration was tuned in-sample, its hyper-parameters were selected on the same perception metric they are then reported on, and the rollout campaign is a fresh measurement those hyper-parameters never saw. Motion prediction is on in every row, and the shipped configuration has the worst measured velocity estimate of the three we tested. Occlusion inflates the landmark error, by about 2.5x on proximal joints and 4x on distal ones, rather than dropping landmarks, because the reference device predicts an occluded joint instead of omitting it. And the pipeline is not finger-accurate, four finger capsules are fused into one segment because no commodity tracker reports finger joints. The 200 ms of latency is adopted and swept rather than calibrated, the rest of the noise model is calibrated.*

#### How perception is created

Each clip replays the sensing pipeline tick by tick. Two virtual depth cameras cast a ray grid into the scene, the returned points are attached to the limbs of the simulated body tracker and coloured by skeleton segment, and each limb is fitted with the capsule the safety layer then treats as the obstacle. When a segment leaves view its capsule is held at its last fit, drawn in amber, rather than dropped. On the left is the headline 40x30 grid, on the right the dense 160x120 grid, the same scene and seed. The denser grid resolves more of the arm and holds fewer segments.

Perception is not free, and closing the second leak described further down cost nothing measurable. On the designed dynamic scenes, safe-success for the whole-arm filter is 50.6% at the headline sparse grid against 63.8% with the true obstacle pose, and penetration is 1.9%. Penetration means strict mesh overlap, the paper's safety definition, scored against ground truth even though the filter plans against the noisy estimate. The gap from 63.8% to 50.6% is a task cost rather than a safety cost. A noisy and partly occluded obstacle estimate makes the barrier harder to satisfy, so the filter holds more standoff and completes fewer places, while penetration stays near the ground-truth level. Where the estimate is wrong it is usually conservative, an unobserved limb is held at its last fit rather than dropped, so the filter keeps the arm safe and pays for it in task progress. The leak-free re-run moved the filter by a handful of episodes in every arm, safe-success from 48.1 to 50.6 on the designed sparse arm and penetration from 2.5 to 1.9, all of it inside the noise this campaign can resolve, so none of it is called an improvement. The one real movement is a guidance row, whose designed-sparse penetration rose from 41.2 to 48.1 (66 episodes against 77), because guidance has no runtime filter and is the method most exposed to a change in the obstacle estimate. The new estimator emits 93 constraint rows instead of 119, but the measured control rate is unchanged at about 18 Hz, so it is cheaper on paper and not in the loop.

How much sensing matters is a condition, not a fixed trade. When the sensor already resolves the obstacle well enough for the motion at hand, extra rays buy only standoff, and standoff costs completions. When it does not, the extra rays buy back the episodes being lost to a wrong obstacle, and there is no trade. On the designed set the two grids are statistically indistinguishable on safe-success, 65 episodes safe under both, 16 lost by going dense and 13 gained, exact McNemar p = 0.71. Every one of those 16 lost episodes failed on task completion rather than collision, each with positive clearance in the dense run. On the tight stress set the effect is real, safe-success 8 lost and 21 gained (p = 0.024), penetration 21 fixed and 4 broken (p = 0.0009). The campaign is not powered to prove equivalence, so the designed-set nulls read as no difference detected rather than as sameness.

#### The leak that survived the de-leak

The July pass closed the geometry leak, where a body the sensor could not fit was emitted as its exact ground-truth capsule. It left a second leak in place, in the association step. The number of obstacle bodies was read from the true topology, and the K-means that assigns cloud points to limbs was re-seeded at the true capsule midpoints on every tick. The module's own docstring had disclosed this the whole time as a deliberate simplification, justified by the claim that segmentation is separable from fit accuracy.

That justification did not survive measurement. Re-running the same clouds with k-means++ seeding, which uses no ground truth, agreed with the ground-truth-seeded labels on only 23.8% of points at the shipped sparse grid. At that density the cloud carried about four points per body, so the seeds effectively were the segmentation. The replacement seeds from noisy skeleton landmarks instead and reaches a measured assignment purity of 85.3% on the shipped human-arm pipeline at the calibrated noise level. A controlled sandbox that isolates the label step scores it even more starkly, 38.0% for the ground-truth-seeded clustering against 96.7% for the skeleton, but those two numbers come from a synthetic four-capsule test rather than the shipped pipeline, and only their direction and mechanism transfer, not their magnitudes.
