## Abstract

Generative motion policies, from diffusion planners to vision-language-action models, enable robots to perform complex manipulation tasks from demonstrations but lack safety guarantees in dynamic environments with novel or moving obstacles. We present a safety framework that augments frozen generative policies with whole-arm collision avoidance through a unified control barrier function formulation. The framework supports both guided sampling during generation and model predictive control filtering during execution. These capabilities avoid moving obstacles without modifying the underlying generative model or assuming a specific robot embodiment. Unlike existing methods that restrict safety to the robot tip or rely on reactive braking, our approach anticipates obstacle motion and reroutes the whole arm while preserving task progress wherever feasible. Experiments with diffusion policies and flow-matching policies across multiple robot platforms show improved safe task completion over representative baselines, with the model predictive control filter the strongest configuration at 63.8% safe task completion.

## Method

The safety layer wraps a **frozen** generative motion policy and enforces whole-arm collision avoidance at execution time, without retraining. It is **generator-agnostic**: one interface wraps a diffusion planner, a flow-matching model, a Diffusion Policy, and a pi0.5 vision-language-action policy. It is **robot-agnostic**: one parametric forward-kinematics and capsule model instantiates the Franka Panda, UR5e, KUKA IIWA, and Kinova Gen3 from a per-robot description.

The robot and the obstacles are over-approximated by **capsules** (a segment plus a radius). The signed clearance `d(q)` is the exact closest-point capsule-to-capsule distance over all robot-obstacle pairs, and the barrier is the margin-shifted clearance `h(q) = d(q) - r_safe`. A conservative capsule radius makes a positive capsule clearance certify a positive mesh clearance. Because the decision input is the joint velocity and `q_{k+1} = q_k + qdot * dt`, the barrier has relative degree one, so a **first-order discrete control barrier function** is well posed and sufficient.

One CBF specification is applied at three points along the generation-to-execution axis:

- **Guidance** at generation time, shaping the sample against the obstacle by the barrier gradient.
- **Filter** at execution time, a model-predictive controller that re-solves a hard, state-feedback problem against the live joint state and the predicted (constant-velocity) obstacle at every tick.
- **Hybrid**, guidance then filter.

Only the execution-time filter repairs the command against the live state and the predicted obstacle, so it is the placement that yields a hard guarantee independent of how the plan was produced.

### Results

We evaluate in Robosuite on the PickPlace-can task. The robot picks a can and places it in a bin while a separate dynamic obstacle, an articulated arm of ten capsule links, moves into the workspace. The obstacle is grasp-triggered and executes one of sixteen curated motions that challenge each link and joint of the arm in turn, so checking only the end effector is structurally insufficient. The headline generator is a Diffusion Policy in absolute joint position, the loop runs at 20 Hz, and the obstacle is crossed with ten seeds.

The filter delivers the guarantee the baselines do not, holding penetration at **zero** across every whole-arm configuration on the designed matrix, while every faithful baseline penetrates the obstacle on a third to three quarters of episodes. The best cell, whole-arm coverage with obstacle prediction on a Diffusion Policy, reaches **63.8% safe task completion at zero penetration**, beating the strongest baseline by roughly 31 points and widening the gap on the harder stress set. The guarantee extends to the carried can through the post-grasp object capsule.

### Ablation: where to enforce safety

The same CBF specification is placed at three points along the generation-to-execution axis: **Guidance** at generation time, the execution-time **Filter** (a model-predictive controller), and **Hybrid**, guidance followed by the filter. Guidance alone shapes the sample against the obstacle pose available at generation time, but by execution the obstacle has moved and the guided plan is stale, so it still penetrates on a third of episodes. The execution-time filter re-solves against the live joint state and the predicted obstacle at every control tick and drops penetration to zero. Hybrid does not improve on the filter alone. ### Perception robustness

The headline experiments hand the filter the true obstacle pose. To measure what the safety layer is worth without one, we replace that oracle with a simulated depth pipeline. Two virtual depth cameras cast rays into the scene, the returned points are clustered, and each cluster is fitted with a capsule that the safety layer treats as the obstacle. A body too sparsely sampled to fit is represented by a proxy built only from the points actually seen, and a body that returns no points at all is absent from the obstacle set. The obstacle velocity that drives the motion-prediction term is a finite difference of the perceived track, not a reading off the simulator. No obstacle geometry and no obstacle velocity is read off the simulator. What the pipeline does still get for free is identity and bookkeeping, namely how many bodies there are, which returned points belong to the obstacle, and where the clustering starts. On hardware that role would be filled by a low-rate detector for identity plus per-tick geometric self-filtering against the robot's own known capsules. One honest caveat on the proxy, observed points lie on the camera-facing surface, so it does not enclose a body's occluded far side. That is the correct semantics, unseen geometry cannot be certified, but it means the proxy is not conservative in the enclosure sense.

#### How perception is created

Each explainer replays a single tick of the sensing pipeline. Two virtual depth cameras cast a ray grid into the scene, the returned points are segmented and clustered, and each cluster is fitted with the capsule that the safety layer then treats as the obstacle. On the left is the headline 40x30 grid, where sparsely sampled bodies are fitted coarsely. On the right is the dense 160x120 grid, where enough rays land that every obstacle capsule gets fitted.

Perception is not free. On the designed dynamic scenes, safe-success for the whole-arm filter falls from 63.8% with the true obstacle pose to 48.1% with the perceived one, and penetration rises from 0.0% to 2.5%. Penetration here means strict mesh overlap, the paper's safety definition, and it is scored against ground truth even though the filter plans against the noisy estimate. The mechanism is visible in the solver. The constraint relaxation the filter has to admit rises by roughly two and a half orders of magnitude (QP slack integral 0.0002 to 0.0710), because a noisy obstacle estimate makes the barrier genuinely hard to satisfy. This is why task completion falls while penetration stays low, the filter keeps the arm safe and pays for it in task progress. The ranking of the methods is unchanged, the filter still leads and still penetrates far less than guidance alone (2.5% against 41.2%). The evaluated runs measure 18.2 Hz, most of which is the simulated ray-cast standing in for a real depth sensor, so on hardware the same loop projects to 29.2 Hz at the headline grid and 26.4 Hz at the dense grid (projections, not hardware measurements).

An earlier version of this section also claimed the obstacle was recovered from onboard sensing rather than privileged state and reported a 1.9-point cost, both wrong at the configuration first shipped, where obstacle bodies the sensor failed to fit were silently replaced by their exact ground-truth geometry and the motion-prediction term read the true velocity. Both leaks are closed and the campaign was re-run.*

#### The stress set, and how much sensing matters

The study above covers the 16 designed dynamic scenes. The 22-pose gold/silver stress set, built so the frozen generator is forced to penetrate, had no perceived counterpart until now. This revises a claim. On the open dynamic scenes, denser sensing moves safe-success 48.1 to 50.6, which is not significant. On the tight stress set it moves 35.9 to 43.6, which is significant (paired McNemar z = 2.97, p about 0.003), and it cuts penetration from 13.6% to 2.7% (z = 4.20, p below 0.0001). The reason is the clearance budget. Ground-truth mean clearance is 174.6 mm on the dynamic set and 43.5 mm on the stress set, while the estimate error is the same order in both. At 175 mm there is little damage to undo, so density shows almost nothing. At 43 mm the same error decides between clearing and overlapping. Sensor resolution matters in proportion to how little clearance a scene leaves. In open scenes it is nearly free. In tight ones it is the difference between avoiding the obstacle and hitting it.

#### Why some scenes collapse under perception

The most revealing result is which scenes break, and why. A scene can be entirely clean under the true obstacle and collapse under perception, and which scenes do that is not predicted by how tight they are. All 30 penetrating episodes in the sparse stress set come from 6 of the 22 poses. The other 16 never penetrate. The per-scene correlation between ground-truth clearance and perceived penetration rate is -0.13, which is nothing. What decides it is whether the sensor can resolve the specific obstacle body the robot approaches in that pose.

Three mechanisms sit behind these, and they respond differently to more sensing. First, a body not seen at all, which is recoverable. Some obstacle capsules return zero points, so the filter holds no constraint for them, and four poses behave this way. Density drives all four to zero penetrations. paper_gold_04 is the clean case. Six of its ten obstacle capsules are unobserved at the sparse grid and none at the dense grid, and the outcome flips from every seed penetrating by 17 to 36 mm to every seed clearing by 38 to 58 mm, with task success going from 0% to 90%. Second, a body that is genuinely occluded, which is only partly recoverable. paper_silver_11 keeps four capsules unobserved even at the dense grid, so penetration and depth both halve but do not close, because more rays cannot see through geometry. Third, a scene with no margin to begin with, which is not a perception problem at all. paper_silver_01 has three of ten seeds at exactly 0.0 mm even with the true obstacle.

Which bodies fail is consistent and explainable. All 30 penetrations land on the obstacle's small distal bodies, the fingers (16 of them), the clavicle (11) and the thumb (3). Those present the least area to the cameras, so they are the first to be lost. The ground-truth control shows the robot approaches the same bodies either way, so the geometry is not the difference. Only under perception do those approaches become overlaps.

One detail inverts a natural intuition and is worth stating plainly. On paper_gold_04 the QP slack the filter admits goes from 0.1 mm at the sparse grid to 67.4 mm at the dense grid. The safe configuration relaxes its constraints far more. At the sparse grid the filter was not straining, because six of its ten obstacle capsules did not exist for it, so it was confidently solving the wrong problem. Low slack is not evidence of safety. It can mean the constraint set is incomplete.
